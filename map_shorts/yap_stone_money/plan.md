## Voiceover (12 lines, 191 words; final Lumean take 64.8 s)
1. If you look at the western Pacific, and zoom in closer, you'll find the island of Yap.
2. About eleven thousand people live here, and their money is giant stone wheels.
3. The smallest are the size of a coin, but the biggest weighs about four tons.
4. And there is no such stone on Yap. It all came from Palau, over four hundred kilometers away.
5. So how did a stone wheel become money?
6. Centuries ago, Yapese sailors crossed the open ocean in canoes to cut stones from the cliffs of Palau.
7. They towed them home on bamboo rafts, and many stones sank on the way.
8. The harder the trip, the more the stone was worth.
9. And here's the twist: you never have to move it. The whole island just remembers who owns it.
10. One huge stone fell into the sea and was never seen again. But everyone agreed it was still there, so people kept trading it.
11. Today they pay with dollars, but the stones still pay for weddings and land.
12. So if a man on Yap says his money is at the bottom of the ocean, he might be telling the truth.

## Facts (checked on Wikipedia, 15 Oct 2026; hedged in the script)
- Rai stones, Yap, Micronesia; disk with a hole; diameter 3.5 cm to 3.6 m, most 30-50 cm; largest standing stone 3.6 m / 4,000 kg (Rumung) [Rai stones].
- Most quarried on Palau, "400 km west of Yap on a straight line"; the voyage route could be >1,000 km [Rai stones]. (The Yap article says ~450 km by canoe; the script says "about four hundred".)
- Quarrying at Metuker ra Bisech appears to start c. 1700 CE; legends say 500-600 years [Rai stones] -> script says "centuries ago".
- Large stones on bamboo rafts set adrift toward Yap with canoes following; stones often lost at sea [Rai stones].
- Ownership by oral history, no need to move the stone; the sunken-stone anecdote is in the article [Rai stones].
- Still used in marriages, land transfers, compensation; USD for everyday use [Rai stones, Yap].
- Yap population 11,377 (2010 census) [Yap].
Caveat to keep: the Wikipedia article notes a 2022 paper arguing some Western accounts of the stones (e.g. Friedman's) contain errors, so the script avoids the "Fed vault" comparison and the O'Keefe legend. The "man at the bottom of the ocean" ending is a joke, stated as "might be".
